Published in 1987 by Emerson & Stern Associates (authored by researcher Don Newkirk), SignFont was developed as a linear, typable orthography for American Sign Language with contributions from the San Diego Deaf community and 40 Deaf and hearing researchers across the country. The SignFont Revival is an ongoing community project revitalizing this system for the modern web.

Historical Roots & The Revival

A Landmark 1987 Orthography. Revitalized for the Modern Web.

Published in 1987 by Emerson & Stern Associates, SignFont was developed to create a written form of Sign Language that is easy to learn, easy to read, and easy to write by hand or computer.

The 1987 Handbook & Collaborative Roots

As documented in the 1987 SignFont Architect Handbook, SignFont was built on the experience of earlier sign writing attempts. Over the course of two years, many contributors from the Deaf community in San Diego, alongside 40 Deaf and hearing researchers, linguists, artists, educators, and writers across the country, contributed ideas and energy to develop the system.

"SignFont is an attempt to create a written form of Sign Language that is easy to learn, easy to read, and easy to write by hand or by computer. Other people have tried to do this before, and we are building on their experience. We ask you to join us as a SignFont Architect to make this writing system work and continue to grow." — Preface, SignFont Architect Handbook (Emerson & Stern Associates, Inc., 1987)

Decades after original 8-bit implementations faded from active use, The SignFont Revival Project is an ongoing community initiative. We are building open-source browser tools, modern Unicode character mapping schemas, interactive dictionaries, and font renderers so that this historical orthography remains accessible, discoverable, and usable today.

  • 1987 · The SignFont Architect Handbook

    Emerson & Stern Associates publishes the SignFont Architect Handbook (authored by Don Newkirk), presenting the alphabet, spelling rules, and dictionary.

  • 1990s · Preservation in Archives

    As early microcomputer character sets became obsolete, SignFont documentation was preserved primarily across academic libraries, microfilms, and scans.

  • Active · Modern Unicode Adaptation

    Unicode character mapping is actively developed on the Signed Conlangs & Linguistics Discord (building upon the Unicode project archive by jan Olipija).

  • Active · The SignFont Revival Project

    An ongoing, community-driven effort prototyping open web tools—interactive dictionaries, virtual keyboards, converters, flashcards, and SVG/PNG rendering suites for modern research and education.

English Preface (Emerson & Stern, 1987)

SignFont is an attempt to create a written form of Sign Language that is easy to learn, easy to read, and easy to write by hand or by computer. Other people have tried to do this before, and we are building on their experience.

We ask you to join us as a SignFont Architect to make this writing system work and continue to grow. During the past two years many people in the Deaf community in San Diego, and 40 Deaf and hearing researchers, linguists, artists, educators, and writers around the country have contributed their ideas and energy to SignFont.

This handbook is designed to help you learn SignFont in the shortest time possible. There are three main sections in the Handbook: the SignFont alphabet (Handshapes, Movements, and so on); a detailed discussion of how to put characters together to spell signs; and a Dictionary of several hundred signs with pronouns, fingerspelling conventions, and numbers.

Please read through the handbook carefully and try the exercises. The answers to the exercises are printed at the end of the "How to Write" section, so you can check immediately to see if you understand the system.

Linguistic Architecture

The Anatomy of a SignFont Word

Every SignFont sign is constructed from 5 modular phonological tiers, reading smoothly from left to right.

1. Handshape Prime
aQ
Curled Fists (Baby-O)
Base hand configuration from the 19 core handshape classes (a–u) with diacritic modifier.
2. Action Surface
!
Palm Side
Active contact surface: palm side (!), back ("), radial edge (%), or interdigital space (+).
3. Body Location
/ 1
Both Temples
Spatial landmark on head or torso: temples (/), nose (3), chin (6), chest (9), or neutral space (_).
4. Motion Vector
H
Circular Path
3D movement trajectory: circular sweep (H), wrist twist (I/J), upward (D), or outward (G).
5. Dynamic Modifier
vy
Dual Hands & Repeated
Both hands identical (v), alternating (w), tense emphasis (z), or repeated (y).
